# Bezos Didn't Build Amazon to Become "Jeff Bezos"

In his essay *Resourcefulness*, Bezos shares a framework that sounds almost too simple:

*"You run into problems, failures, things that don't work. You need to back up and try again."*

But buried in the same chapter is a line that explains everything about why he even tried in the first place:

> **"When I'm eighty, I want to have minimized the number of regrets I have in life. And most of our regrets are acts of omission — the things we didn't try, the paths we didn't walk. Those are the ones that haunt us."**

This isn't about success. It's about regret avoidance.

---

You can hear the exact same idea, framed differently, in Steve Jobs's 2005 Stanford commencement speech:

*"If today were the last day of my life, would I want to do what I'm about to do today? And whenever the answer has been 'no' for too many days in a row, I know I need to change something."*

Jobs asked himself this question every morning for 33 years. Not "am I rich enough?" Not "am I famous enough?" Just: *if this were it — the last day — would I be proud of how I spent it?*

Bezos asked a version of the same question when he was at DE Shaw. He had money. He had status. He had a clear trajectory on Wall Street. His boss — David Shaw — took him for a walk in Central Park, listened to the internet-bookstore idea, and said honestly:

*"This is a great idea — for someone who doesn't already have a good job."*

Kind feedback. Brutally honest. And Bezos almost agreed. He gave himself 48 hours. And then he thought about being eighty. He thought about the regret. And he quit.

---

Here's what I find profound: Bezos wasn't trying to become *someone*. He was trying to avoid becoming someone who didn't try.

This connects to a concept from the *Diamond Sutra*, a central text in Mahayana Buddhism: **attachment is what controls you.** The moment you're attached to a label — "successful hedge fund VP," "respected professional," "person who makes safe choices" — you're no longer free. The attachment owns you.

Non-attachment doesn't mean you don't care. It means you're not trapped by the identity you've built. You can walk away from a good job. You can risk looking foolish. You can try something that might fail — because failing at something you attempted is less painful than succeeding at something you never wanted.

---

Now, let me be honest about something.

This is a story told by a winner. Bezos built Amazon into a trillion-dollar company — of course the "regret minimization framework" sounds brilliant in retrospect. History is written by the victors. If Amazon had failed in 1999 like Pets.com, this would be a cautionary tale about hubris, not an inspirational one about courage.

But here's what I still take from it: **the framework doesn't depend on the outcome.**

You don't need to build Amazon for the regret-minimization idea to be useful. You just need to ask yourself, honestly: *is there something I'm not trying because I'm attached to a label — my job title, my reputation, my comfort zone?*

Most regrets are acts of omission. Not the things you tried and failed at. The things you never tried at all.

**Focus on the process, not the end result.** The label is a trap. The game is the point.
