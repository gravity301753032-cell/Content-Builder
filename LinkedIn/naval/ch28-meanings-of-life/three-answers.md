# Three Answers to the Meaning of Life — LinkedIn

I've been reading Naval Ravikant's Almanack. Chapter 28 opens with a question most people spend their entire lives avoiding: *what is the meaning of life?*

Naval doesn't give one answer. He gives three. Each one contradicts the last. I think that's the point.

---

**Answer 1: It's personal. You have to find your own.**

Nobody can give you the answer. Not Buddha, not Naval, not me. You have to sit with the question yourself. It might take years. When you find an answer you're satisfied with, it will become fundamental to how you live.

This is the self-help answer. Empowering, but incomplete. It puts all the weight on you.

---

**Answer 2: There is no meaning.**

The universe is 13.8 billion years old. You were dead for all of it. You will be dead for the next 70 billion years until the heat death of the universe.

Everything you build will fade. The human race will disappear. Mars will disappear. Nobody will remember you past a few generations — artist, poet, conqueror, pauper, doesn't matter. You're just passing through.

And if there *was* a cosmic meaning, you'd just ask "why is *that* the meaning?" and then "why that?" — infinite regress. Physicist Richard Feynman called it "turtles all the way down."

No fundamental purpose. No objective meaning. You were born without consent into a universe that doesn't care.

This sounds bleak. But here's the flip: if nothing has built-in meaning, you can't fail. You're free. The pressure to "find your purpose" or "leave a legacy" — all made up. If nobody remembers you 30 years after you're gone, that's not tragedy. That's permission to live for yourself.

---

**Answer 3: We are all part of one thing.**

This is where Naval brings in physics.

The second law of thermodynamics: entropy only increases. The universe moves toward disorder, toward "heat death" — a state where all particles are evenly distributed, all boundaries dissolve, everything becomes one undifferentiated whole.

Here's the twist: living systems *accelerate* this. Every time you act, every civilization you build, every piece of art you create — you're pushing the universe faster toward oneness.

> *"What we do as living systems accelerates getting to that state. You're pushing us towards this point where we end up as one thing."*

In other words: we are not separate from the universe. We are the universe's mechanism for returning to itself.

---

This is where I stopped and sat with it for a while.

Answer 3 reminded me of Zhuangzi, the Daoist philosopher. In *Qiwulun* (齊物論, The Equality of Things), he argues that all distinctions — right and wrong, self and other, you and me — are illusions created by perspective. Everything is one whole. We just can't see it from where we're standing.

I have my own metaphor for this.

Imagine a massive painting. Every color on that canvas — yellow, red, blue — is a person. Yellow obsesses over painting the most beautiful sunflower. Red agonizes over the perfect rose. Blue keeps asking: "Am I doing enough? Is my corner meaningful?"

None of them realize: they're *already* part of the painting. Their very existence on the canvas is the meaning. Yellow doesn't need to paint the sunflower. Yellow is yellow, and that's enough. The masterpiece needs every color — not just the ones that "achieve" something.

We are the colors. The world is the canvas.

Your existence is not a problem waiting for a purpose. You're already part of something bigger than you can perceive. You can't *not* have impact. You can't *not* matter. The only variable is whether you're aware of it.

---

So where does this leave me?

Answer 2 says there's no meaning. Answer 3 says we're all part of one thing and our very existence is meaning. These sound contradictory. They're not.

They're the same truth seen from different distances.

From far away (cosmic scale): nothing matters. Zoom in (your life, your relationships, your corner of the canvas): everything matters, because you're part of the whole.

The practical question I'm left with: given that I'm here on this canvas, with these specific colors and this specific 80-year window — how do I decide what to do?

That's where values come in. Next post.
