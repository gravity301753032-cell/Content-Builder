# Your Values Are Your Decision Engine — LinkedIn

In my last post, I landed on this: after wrestling with Naval's three answers to the meaning of life, the practical question is — how do I decide what to do with my 80 years?

Naval's answer in Chapter 29: live by your values.

He lists three: honesty, long-term thinking, and avoiding angry people. Simple enough. But I kept reading and realized he wasn't really giving a list. He was describing a *decision engine*.

When your values are clear, you don't agonize over choices. The framework does the work for you. A decision that violates your values gets rejected automatically — no mental energy spent.

The value that landed hardest for me: honesty.

---

Not honesty as in "don't lie." Honesty as in *be the same person in every room*.

Naval calls it being your authentic self. Wang Yangming, a Ming-dynasty philosopher, called it 知行合一 — the unity of knowing and doing. When your inner state matches your outward actions, you stop performing and start being. When you're honest with yourself, you're automatically honest with everyone else. You don't have to "try." It's not a tactic. It's a state.

Here's why this matters beyond philosophy.

I spent years in accounting and finance. I watched people build careers on carefully managed impressions. It works — for a while. But deception doesn't compound. A founder who lies to investors eventually runs out of investors. A founder who lies to themselves about churn or runway eventually runs out of company. You can't build long-term anything on a short-term foundation.

A liar can never succeed at long-term games. Not as a moral judgment — as game mechanics. Trust is the currency that makes compound interest possible.

---

This got me thinking about decisions differently.

Naval frames everything as "long-term vs short-term." But I kept coming back to a problem: what counts as "long-term" when your entire life is a rounding error against a 13.8-billion-year-old universe? From that distance, *everything* is short-term.

So I reframed it.

I grew up playing a lot of games. GTA, mostly. In any game, you don't skip side quests just because they're short. Side quests are part of the experience. What you *do* avoid are the missions that get you killed.

Same in life. The real question isn't "is this long-term or short-term?" The question is: **does this choice cost me HP?**

Some choices damage your ability to keep playing. Lying costs you reputation — eventually you run out of NPCs who trust you. Burning bridges costs you future options. Neglecting your health costs you turns on the board.

Other choices are short but harmless. Coffee with someone you'll never do business with. A weekend off. A project that probably won't scale — but won't hurt either.

Short-term isn't the problem. Self-destructive is.

---

There's another layer here that took me a while to articulate.

Naval says avoid angry people. I get it. Anger is exhausting. But here's what I've learned: expecting everyone to play long-term games is itself a form of attachment.

In Buddhist thought — and in my own life — I try not to label people as "good" or "bad." Some people operate on short timelines. They're not villains. They're just NPCs in your game. You don't need to hate them or change them. You just need to know what they are, and not give them main-character energy.

Your honesty is a standard you hold yourself to — not a demand you make of the world.

---

Naval talks about finding a partner who shares your values. He mentions Charlie Munger's line: "To find a good partner, be a good partner." He also says something interesting about his wife — that when they met, he didn't feel like he deserved her.

Here's a man who wrote an entire book about self-determination, about creating your own reality. And when he talks about love, he *surrenders*. It's the one place in the book where the armor comes off.

I think there's a truth in that. You can build your values. You can refine your decision engine. But in relationships — the ones that matter — you don't get to optimize your way in. You just show up as yourself and see if it fits.

Most relationships, if we're honest, are short-term. You can tell from the beginning. The motivation is sex, or money, or convenience. Nobody says it out loud, but it's there in the unspoken agreement. These aren't moral failures. They're just not long-term plays. Know what you're signing up for.

---

So here's the framework I'm working with:

**Don't ask "is this long-term or short-term?" Ask "does this cost me HP?"**

Your values are the auto-pilot. Honesty — real honesty, the 知行合一 kind — is the foundation that makes everything else compound. And you don't need everyone to be a main character. Some people are side quests. Know the difference.

But here's the uncomfortable part: even with the clearest values and the best decision engine, you still don't control the future.

More on that in the next one.
