Part 4 of 5: Where the model stops and a person starts.

The model is very good at the part of your work that has one right answer. It is much worse at knowing where that part ends.

Two good accountants can disagree about whether a modified contract is a new contract. That is not a defect in the model. That is the actual work, and it is not going away.

Which makes a skill that does not have a name yet: writing the boundary. For each decision, you need to state the point where it stops being a rule and starts being judgement, and the condition that means stop here and escalate to me.

This is different from writing the rule. Writing the rule says what to do. Writing the boundary says when not to do it.

And you cannot skip it, because the model will not write it for you. It has no concept of the grey zone, so it will pick an answer in the middle of it, confidently, and hand you a number that is defensible and wrong.

Here is what this looks like in practice. Take one decision your team makes often. Write the rule. Then write the three conditions that mean the machine stops and a person takes over. Keep them short enough that a reviewer can apply them at a glance, and specific enough that two reviewers would make the same call.

Then watch what happens to the queue. Most teams find their exceptions are not random. They cluster on a handful of judgement calls, which means you now know exactly which judgements you still need people for, and which ones you do not.

The work does not disappear. It moves to the part that was always the hard part.

What is the first decision you would hand over, and what is the first one you would not?
