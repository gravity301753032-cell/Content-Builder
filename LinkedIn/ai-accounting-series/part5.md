Part 5 of 5: Who signs when the model drafts.

None of this matters if there is no trail.

An auditor does not ask whether the number is right. They ask how you arrived at it. Which means, for every entry: the rule that was applied, the data it read, the output the model produced, who approved it, and which version of your policy was in force at the time.

That last one is why you cannot bake your policy into the model. A policy you trained in last year is a policy you will be correcting this year, and if the system cannot tell you which version produced a number, the trail is decoration.

So the trail is not paperwork. It is the thing that lets you let a machine draft in the first place.

And when that exists, the human signature changes shape. You stop signing for every entry. You sign for the control: here is the rule, here is the boundary, here is the exception queue, here is the trail, here is what I reviewed and what I escalated.

That is a harder signature, not a lighter one. It cannot be delegated, and it cannot be produced by seniority. It has to be earned by evidence.

And here is the part of this nobody wants to say out loud. The junior work that trained a generation of accountants, coding transactions, reconciling, drafting entries, is exactly the work the model just did at 92% accuracy. So the rung of the ladder that used to teach people how to think is disappearing, and the profession has to build the next one deliberately, or there is no next generation.

Which means the best use of your juniors is not the work the model does faster. It is learning to write the rules, set the boundaries and sign the trail. That is the work that cannot be delegated, so somebody has to learn it.

Your signature is about to become the scarcest thing your finance team has. The only question is whether anyone is being trained to hold it.

What part of your accounting judgement would you trust a model to make today, and who signs when it is wrong?
